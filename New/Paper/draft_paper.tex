\begin{abstract}
\noindent Environmental provisions (EPs) are now ubiquitous in preferential trade
agreements, yet evidence on their trade effects rests on aggregate data. I ask whether the
EPs in China's 2000--2015 agreements shifted the \emph{composition} of its exports toward
green and away from dirty products, using 45.8 million
firm--product--destination--year customs transactions. A triple-difference compares green
and dirty products to neutral ones within firm--destination--year cells, so that
firm--destination--year fixed effects absorb the agreement itself. The green margin is a
null: stable across six estimation designs and robust to wild cluster bootstrap and
permutation inference, with bootstrap bounds that rule out effects larger than roughly one
quarter of the benchmark estimate in the aggregate literature. The dirty margin is a
textbook false positive --- overwhelming asymptotically ($p<0.001$), it survives neither
the bootstrap ($p=0.07$) nor permutation inference ($p=0.28$), and a leave-one-out exercise
shows a single destination carries it. The central reading is about content, not chapters:
the two World Bank sub-indices with a direct trade mechanism are perfectly collinear
in-sample and non-zero in only three treated country--years. China's chapters were thin and
cooperation-only, which makes the null consistent with --- not contrary to ---
\citet{brandi2020} and \citet{abman2024}, whose effects come from specific, enforceable
clauses.\\[4pt]
\textbf{Keywords:} trade agreements, environmental provisions, export composition, China.\\
\textbf{JEL:} F13, F14, F18, Q56.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================================

Nearly every trade agreement signed in the last two decades contains environmental
language. The TREND database \citep{morin2018} codes roughly 300 distinct types of
environmental provisions (EPs) across more than 700 preferential trade agreements (PTAs),
and the depth of this content has grown steadily. Whether these provisions have real
effects --- on trade flows or on environmental outcomes --- is a question that the
authoritative survey of the trade--environment literature flags as open
\citep{copeland2022}. Two recent papers provide the sharpest evidence to date.
\citet{brandi2020} combine TREND with aggregate bilateral trade flows and find that
trade-restrictive EPs reduce the dirty share, and liberal EPs raise the green share, of
developing-country exports. \citet{abman2024} show that forest- and biodiversity-specific
provisions fully offset the deforestation that otherwise follows entry into force of a
regional trade agreement.

This paper brings the question to the world's largest exporter with the most granular data
available: the universe of Chinese customs transactions, observed at the
firm--product(HS6)--destination--year level (49.2 million observations, roughly 462{,}000
exporting firms). Between 2000 and 2015 China signed around 14 PTAs --- covering 25
destination economies --- whose environmental content ranges from essentially none (the
Bangkok Agreement) to substantial chapters (New Zealand 2008, Switzerland 2014, Korea
2015). I ask whether deeper environmental content shifted the \emph{composition} of
Chinese exports toward green goods and away from dirty goods, relative to neutral
products, within the same firm, destination, and year.

Why transaction-level data, when the existing evidence is aggregate? Because a
composition effect measured on country-pair flows and one measured inside a firm are not
the same object. In aggregate data the green share of a country's exports can rise
without any exporter changing its own product mix --- it is enough that different firms,
or different products, enter and exit the market. That is a real phenomenon, but it is
not the reallocation the policy argument for environmental chapters has in mind, and it
is mechanically vulnerable to compositional churn. Firm--product--destination--year
transactions let me hold the firm, the destination, and the year fixed and ask whether
the \emph{same} exporter tilted its basket, which is a strictly more demanding test of
the same hypothesis. The cost is that a firm-level design cannot be compared with the
aggregate literature term by term; the paper pays that cost explicitly by also estimating
the design on a product--destination--year panel, and by showing that this collapsed
panel is algebraically identical to the micro regression under the same fixed effects
(Appendix~\ref{app:pddt}), so the two levels of aggregation can be read on one scale.

The focus on composition, rather than on export volumes, is dictated by identification.
EP depth in Chinese agreements varies at the destination--year level, enters in force
together with the agreement itself, and afterwards almost never changes (only three
destinations exhibit within-country variation). Any level effect of EP depth on trade is
therefore observationally equivalent to the effect of the agreement, and of agreement
depth in general --- a collinearity that no fixed-effects structure can undo. I document
this formally with a saturation ladder: coefficients on EP depth shrink monotonically to
a precise zero as fixed effects absorb the level of the trade relationship, the signature
of selection into agreements rather than of a causal effect
\citep{bertrand2004, abadie2023}. The composition question, by contrast, is identified:
within a firm--destination--year cell, the agreement affects green, dirty, and neutral
products symmetrically, so their \emph{differential} response to EP depth isolates the
environmental content. The design follows the logic of the classic
industry-$\times$-country interaction of \citet{rajan1998}, and mirrors the contrast that
\citet{abman2024} exploit between agreements that do and do not contain specific
environmental content, holding the existence of an agreement fixed. The analogy is structural, not literal: \citeauthor{rajan1998}'s industries are
sorted by a technological trait (external finance dependence) that is fixed across
countries, whereas green and dirty status here is a \emph{policy}-defined product
classification (OECD and Mani--Wheeler lists), a distinction worth flagging even though
it does not vary with the agreement and so cannot itself confound identification.

The answer has two halves, and it is worth stating them in the same words each time they
recur. On the green margin the paper reports a \emph{bounded null}: an estimate
indistinguishable from zero, with bootstrap bounds that rule out effects larger than
roughly a quarter of the benchmark estimate in the aggregate literature and say nothing
below that. On the dirty margin it reports a \emph{non-result}: an apparent effect that
no form of robust inference sustains, which is a different object from a precisely
estimated zero and is not claimed as one. Six independent estimation
designs --- the full firm-level panel, a product--destination--year collapsed panel, and
four control-group subsamples that probe the stability of the coefficient across
comparison sets --- return an EP$\times$green coefficient between $-0.0009$ and
$-0.0046$ (in log points per provision), never remotely significant, with standard errors
around 0.003--0.007. The dynamic version shows flat differential pre-trends and no break
at entry into force. Inference is conducted at three levels --- destination-clustered
asymptotics, wild cluster bootstrap \citep{cameron2008}, and permutation tests that
reshuffle entire EP profiles across treated destinations \citep{fisher1935,bertrand2004}
--- because only 23 destinations (and effectively 14 agreements) are treated. A
negative coefficient on EP$\times$dirty (asymptotic $p<0.001$ in the collapsed
panel) illustrates exactly why this discipline matters: its wild-bootstrap p-value is
0.07, permutation inference on the exact estimating equation gives $p=0.28$, a coarser
aggregate version of the same permutation flips its sign ($p=0.49$), the TREND index never
confirms it, and a leave-one-out exercise shows it is carried by a single destination
(Australia, $p=0.24$ once excluded); South Korea, the destination whose removal
previously killed the result, now leaves it borderline ($p=0.09$). No form of robust
inference sustains it. The joint
F-test on all four composition interactions in the full firm-level panel is $p=0.31$ (WB
index) and $p=0.71$ (TREND index).

The null is informative rather than disappointing, and its central reading is about
\emph{content}, not about environmental provisions in general. Decomposing the indices
reveals that Chinese EPs of the period bundle provisions so tightly that the two World
Bank sub-indices with a direct trade mechanism --- green-goods liberalization and
environmental standards --- are \emph{perfectly} collinear in-sample ($\rho = 1.00$) and
non-zero in only three treated country--years; most environmental content is soft
cooperation language with no trade mechanism at all. Where \citet{brandi2020} find
effects, they are driven by explicitly trade-restrictive or trade-liberalizing clauses;
the Chinese agreements of 2000--2015 contained almost none of either. Read this way, the
null is not a contrast with \citet{brandi2020} but what their own heterogeneity predicts
for agreements of this composition --- and, on the same logic, compatible with
\citet{abman2024}'s finding that specific, enforceable provisions do move outcomes: China's
chapters were neither. Two further facts reinforce this reading. First, the estimate is
precise: obtained on the near-universe of transactions of the world's dominant exporter,
it firmly rejects economically meaningful composition effects (say, half the magnitudes
in \citealp{brandi2020}), so the null is not merely a failure to reject. Second, it is
stable across control groups --- an artifact of the comparison set would move as the
comparison set moves, and it does not.

This paper contributes to three literatures. The first studies the trade effects of what
is \emph{inside} trade agreements rather than whether one exists. \citet{hofmann2017}
built the World Bank coding of provision-level content used here, and \citet{dur2014} the
DESTA index of overall agreement depth that this paper uses as an alternative control;
both establish that agreements differ enormously in content at similar headline
coverage. \citet{neri2023} show that deeper agreements affect exporters heterogeneously,
and \citet{baccini2017} that the gains are distributed unevenly across firms --- results
that motivate looking at composition inside the firm rather than at aggregate flows. This
paper adds the observation that content variation can be too bundled to identify, which is
a limit on the whole research program and not a feature of one dataset.

The second is the trade--environment nexus. \citet{cherniwchan2017} shows, with
within-plant US data around NAFTA, that trade liberalization changes emissions through the
behaviour of individual producers rather than only through reallocation across them;
\citet{shapiro2021} documents that trade policy is itself systematically tilted against
clean goods; \citet{dechezlepretre2017} survey the competitiveness effects of environmental
regulation, the mechanism through which EPs would have to operate. \citet{copeland2022}
survey the field and flag the effectiveness of environmental provisions as open. This
paper contributes firm-level evidence on the export side of that question, where the
existing work is plant-level and domestic.

The third is the effectiveness of environmental clauses specifically.
\citet{brandi2020} find that environmental provisions raise the green and lower the dirty
share of developing-country exports, using aggregate bilateral flows and the TREND coding
of \citet{morin2018}; \citet{abman2024} find that forest- and biodiversity-specific
provisions offset deforestation following entry into force; \citet{baghdadi2013} find
convergence in emissions among partners whose agreements contain environmental clauses.
The common thread in the positive findings is specificity: effects come from clauses with
an identifiable mechanism. This paper is the mirror image of that thread --- a large,
well-powered sample whose clauses lack such content, and which correspondingly shows
nothing.
Methodologically, it offers a template for honest inference when treatment varies across
few clusters \citep{cameron2008, abadie2023, mackinnon2017} and for control-group
stability analysis in triple-difference designs.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background} describes
the agreements and the data. Section~\ref{sec:strategy} presents the empirical strategy.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the main results, Section~\ref{sec:robust} the
robustness battery, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background and data}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Chinese PTAs and their environmental content}

Between 2000 and 2015, PTAs signed by China and entering into force cover 25 destination
economies. Table~\ref{tab:treatment} lists them. Three features of this treatment
landscape discipline everything that follows. First, the ASEAN agreement (2005) covers
eleven destinations with identical values of every EP index: the effective number of
agreements is roughly 14, not 25. Second, EP content is fixed at signature: only three
destinations (notably South Korea, which moved from the shallow Bangkok Agreement to the
deep 2015 FTA) display within-country variation over time, and in no case does depth
ever fall --- there is no treatment reversal in the sample, so estimators built for
treatment switching on and off are not required here. Third, Hong Kong and Macao
(CEPA, 2003) are entrep\^ot economies whose recorded flows are dominated by re-exports;
they account for half of the export value to treated destinations and are excluded from
the main specification (robustness with inclusion reported below). Following
\citet{feenstra2004}, a large share of ``Chinese exports'' to Hong Kong are in practice
intermediated and re-exported to third countries at a substantial markup, so treating
Hong Kong--Macao as a genuine final destination would misattribute third-country demand
shocks to the bilateral relationship the fixed effects are meant to absorb.

\subsection{Customs data and product classification}

The trade data are the universe of Chinese export customs transactions 2000--2015,
aggregated to the firm--HS6--destination--year level: 49.2 million observations
(45.8 million once Hong Kong and Macao are dropped), about 462{,}000 firms, roughly
5{,}000 HS6 products and 236 destinations. The outcome is the log of export value; the
panel identifiers used for fixed effects (firm--product--destination,
firm--destination--year, product--year) are precomputed.
Export values enter in logs with no trimming or winsorization;
Section~\ref{sec:robust}'s inference battery, which is design-based rather than
moment-based, is the primary guard against influential observations.

\emph{Green} products are the 248 HS6 codes of the OECD Combined List of Environmental
Goods (CLEG) as compiled in \citet{sauvage2014} (verified match of 246/248
against the project's source file, with the single discrepancy a granularity split of
one HS heading into two sub-codes). Because the customs panel uses the HS1996 vintage
while the OECD list is native HS2012, the list was translated to HS1996: 246 of the 248
codes have a unique 1:1 concordance and are translated, while the two that do not
(871411 and 871419, the granularity split noted above) are retained at their original
codes and flagged rather than dropped. Export-value continuity checks around the 2007
revision boundary find no suspicious break on any translated code.\footnote{As a check
on the classification itself rather than its translation, the main specification was
re-estimated restricting green products to the 54 codes of the APEC Environmental Goods
List \citep{sauvage2014}, on which explicit multilateral political consensus exists and
which form a complete subset of the OECD list. With the WB index the green coefficient flips sign to $+0.0050$ (s.e.\ 0.0127, $p=0.69$), with the standard error roughly doubled, as expected from an 80\% reduction in the green sample; with TREND it moves from $+0.0018$ to $+0.0032$ ($p=0.13$), same sign and similar precision. Neither is distinguishable from zero: the null is not an artifact of borderline products in the broader list.} \emph{Dirty} products are the HS6 codes belonging to the classic
pollution-intensive sectors of \citet{mani1998} and \citet{low1992} --- pulp and paper,
industrial chemicals, petroleum refining, iron and steel, non-ferrous metals (plus
cement in an extended list) --- mapped to HS1996 through the official WITS/UNSD
HS1996--ISIC Rev.3 correspondence: 1{,}139 codes. Seventeen codes appearing in both
lists (e.g.\ insulation materials: produced by dirty industries, used for environmental
purposes) are assigned to the green list, which is product-curated; the two categories
are mutually exclusive. Green products account for 11.5\% of firm-level observations and
dirty products for 7.0\%; the remaining ``neutral'' products form the comparison group.

\subsection{Estimation samples and control-group subsamples}\label{sec:samples}

The analysis uses four estimation objects built from the same underlying panel, each
answering a different question. The \emph{full firm-level panel} (45.8 million
firm--HS6--destination--year observations once Hong Kong and Macao are dropped) is the
main object; its singleton-surviving estimation sample is characterized in
Section~\ref{sec:strategy}. The \emph{collapsed panel} aggregates it to
HS6--destination--year cells (3.77 million; outcome: within-cell mean of log exports,
regressions weighted by cell size) and serves both as a computational workhorse for
the inference battery and as an internal cross-validation --- its fixed-effects
structure preserves the identifying logic but not the firm dimension, a difference
Section~\ref{sec:strategy} quantifies. The \emph{zero-filled
PPML grid} (8.2 million cells) adds the combinations with no recorded trade, opening
the extensive margin. The \emph{within-firm share panel} (13.3 million
firm--destination--year observations) recasts the outcome as the green share of each
firm's export basket to a destination; it enters as descriptive evidence only, for the
identification reason given in Section~\ref{sec:robust}.

Because the triple-difference compares green and dirty products to \emph{neutral}
ones, the credibility of the comparison set can be probed directly. Four
control-group subsamples, each tightening the comparison in the
direction of a specific threat:

% ============================================================================
\section{Empirical strategy}\label{sec:strategy}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{Why a level effect is not identifiable}

The natural first specification --- regressing log exports on EP depth with fixed
effects --- is uninformative here, and I document this as a diagnostic rather than as a
result. EP depth varies at the destination--year level and switches on with the
agreement; with $\sim$14 agreements and near-zero within-destination variation,
``environmental depth'' and ``having a (deep) agreement'' cannot be told apart. The
saturation ladder makes the point empirically: moving from sparse
(firm--product--destination + year) to saturated (adding product--time and pair-level
interactive effects) structures, the EP-depth coefficient falls monotonically from small
positive and starred to an exact, precisely estimated zero. The pattern ---
significance that lives only in under-saturated, finely-clustered specifications --- is
the classic signature of selection combined with understated standard errors
\citep{bertrand2004, goodmanbacon2021}. One consequence is worth stating plainly: this
design cannot supply the kind of first-stage ``bite'' evidence --- a demonstration that
signing an EP-laden PTA moved some intermediate outcome, such as aggregate trade
volumes, before composition is examined --- that would normally open a difference-in-
differences case, because that level effect is exactly what is confounded with the
agreement. The saturation ladder is offered as the closest available substitute: it
shows that whatever the agreement did at the level of trade, EP depth specifically is
not separably identified there, which is why the paper's evidence is built entirely on
the composition margin instead.

\subsection{The triple-difference on composition}

The target parameter is an average treatment effect on the treated: the effect of one
additional unit of environmental depth on the gap between green (or dirty) and neutral
log export value, within firm--destination--year cells that experience treatment: in
potential-outcomes terms, $\beta_1 = E[\,(y_{fgpdt}(1) - y_{fgpdt}(0)) - (y_{fnpdt}(1) -
y_{fnpdt}(0))\mid d,t \text{ treated}\,]$, where $y(1)$ and $y(0)$ denote log exports of
green ($g$) versus neutral ($n$) products under, respectively, deeper and unchanged
environmental content, holding the agreement itself fixed. This is a per-cell (per
firm--destination--year), not a per-firm, average. The collapsed-panel implementation
aggregates the firm dimension with cell-count weights, so that destinations or years with
more underlying transactions contribute proportionally more evidence; the weights are the
cell counts of underlying transactions, so the weighted cell-level regression is
algebraically identical to the unweighted micro-level regression under the same fixed
effects (Section~\ref{sec:strategy} verifies this to seven significant figures).
Weighting is thus not a modelling choice but the condition for that equivalence, and no
weighting is by the outcome itself. The main specification is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
\ln x_{fpdt} \;=\; \beta_1\, EP_{dt}\times g_p \;+\; \beta_2\, EP_{dt}\times b_p
\;+\; \gamma_1\, TD_{dt}\times g_p \;+\; \gamma_2\, TD_{dt}\times b_p
\;+\; \theta_{fpd} \;+\; \theta_{fdt} \;+\; \theta_{pt} \;+\; \varepsilon_{fpdt},
\end{equation}
where $f$ indexes firms, $p$ HS6 products, $d$ destinations, $t$ years; $g_p$ and $b_p$
are the green and dirty indicators, $EP_{dt}$ is environmental depth and $TD_{dt}$
non-environmental agreement depth. The firm--destination--year effects $\theta_{fdt}$
are the key: they absorb everything that varies at the level of the firm's relationship
with a market in a year --- including the agreement itself, market size, demand shocks,
and selection into agreements. Identification comes from comparing green (and dirty)
products to neutral ones \emph{within} the same firm--destination--year, as EP content
varies across destinations and over time; $\theta_{pt}$ absorbs global product-level
shocks (e.g.\ the world solar boom) and $\theta_{fpd}$ the level of specialization.
Mechanically, $\theta_{fdt}$ places a separate intercept on every firm--destination--year
cell: any variable constant within that cell --- the agreement dummy, its overall depth,
destination demand, and the non-random selection of destinations into agreements --- is
perfectly collinear with those intercepts and drops out. Selection into agreements can
therefore contaminate $\beta_1$ only if it operates \emph{differentially} on green versus
neutral products within the same firm--destination--year, the residual threat that the
destination-trend exercise of Section~\ref{sec:robust} targets.
The $TD$ interactions separate environmental content from general agreement depth, the
same role the DESTA index \citep{dur2014} plays in \citet{brandi2020}; because
\texttt{TotalDepth} counts all non-environmental provisions rather than isolating
green-relevant ones (e.g.\ subsidy disciplines, technical-standard harmonization), it is
an imperfect proxy for the depth of agreement content that is genuinely orthogonal to
the environmental chapter. An imperfectly measured control does not attenuate the
coefficient on the variable of interest in a signable direction: it under-controls, and
$\beta_1$ absorbs some of the effect of general depth with no guarantee about the sign.
Rather than assert a direction, Section~\ref{sec:robust} reports the coefficient under
four different depth controls --- including none at all --- and shows that the point
estimate moves within a band narrower than one standard error
(Table~\ref{tab:depthbounds}). That is evidence about the direction, where an appeal to
attenuation would only have been an assumption. The correlation is substantial and
should be stated plainly: across the 223 treated destination--years in the estimation
sample, WB EP depth and non-environmental TotalDepth correlate at $0.91$ in the raw data
and $0.96$ after destination and year demeaning (the variation the design actually
uses); the raw-data VIF of $5.8$ is somewhat higher than the maximum of $4.6$ that
\citet{brandi2020} report. The EP$\times$green coefficient is therefore identified from
the modest share of environmental-depth variation that is orthogonal to general
agreement depth --- a limitation that the clustered standard errors already price in
(it is one reason the WB confidence intervals are wider than the TREND ones: the TREND
count is far less collinear with TotalDepth, at $0.50$ raw and $0.85$ within). That two
codings with such different degrees of overlap with general depth return the same null
is itself reassuring: a result driven by the inability to separate environmental from
general content would be expected to differ across them. Standard errors are clustered
by destination, the level of treatment variation \citep{abadie2023}.

One qualification is due, and it concerns the \emph{estimator}, not the target; it matters for how $\beta_1$ should be
read. Environmental depth is a \emph{continuous} dose that switches on at
\emph{staggered} dates, and under that combination the two-way fixed-effects coefficient
is not in general the ATT defined above: it is a weighted average of dose-specific and
cohort-specific effects whose weights need not be convex, and comparisons across dose
levels are additionally exposed to selection into dose that parallel trends alone does
not rule out \citep{callaway2024, goodmanbacon2021, dechaisemartin2020}. This paper does
not implement a continuous-dose robust estimator; the Sun--Abraham event study of
Appendix~\ref{app:sunab} addresses only the staggered-timing half of the problem, and it
does so on a binarized treatment. Two considerations limit how much this costs here.
Estimating a null makes the weighting problem less consequential than it would be for a
non-zero estimate --- non-convex weights distort the aggregation of heterogeneous
effects, but a weighted average of effects that are individually near zero stays near
zero unless the underlying effects are large and offsetting, which the sub-index and
leave-one-out evidence gives no sign of. And the permutation test of
Section~\ref{sec:strategy} is agnostic about the weighting entirely: it asks whether the
observed coefficient, whatever weighted average it represents, is unusual relative to the
same statistic computed under randomly reassigned EP profiles. $\beta_1$ is therefore
best read as a weighted average of composition responses across doses and cohorts, not as
a single structural ATT, and a continuous-dose estimator in the spirit of
\citet{callaway2024} remains the natural next step for this design.

A further identification concern works in a conservative direction. If deeper
environmental chapters are more likely to be signed with destinations where consumer or
regulatory demand for green goods is \emph{already} rising --- a plausible selection
story --- then $EP_{dt}$ is positively correlated with an omitted destination-specific
green-demand trend, which would bias $\beta_1$ upward, toward finding a spurious
positive effect on green exports. Because the estimated $\beta_1$ is a precise zero
rather than positive, this channel implies the true effect (if negative or zero) is, if
anything, understated by the null: the null is a conservative, not a lenient, reading of
the data.

Two complementary implementations address the computational burden of three
high-dimensional fixed effects on 46 million rows. The full panel is estimated with
iterative singleton removal \citep{correia2017}, which eliminates 24.3 million
uninformative observations. The surviving sample is not a selected sliver: it retains
47\% of observations but 70\% of aggregate export value (the removed observations are
predominantly one-off, small flows), and the product composition is essentially
unchanged (green products are 11.5\% of observations before removal and 11.9\% after;
dirty products 7.0\% and 6.4\%). Within the estimation sample, the composition contrast
is identified in the treated firm--destination--year cells that contain both a green
(or dirty) and a neutral product: 26\% of treated cells for the green margin and 12\%
for the dirty margin, with a median of one green (dirty) product per identifying cell
and three products per treated cell overall --- a reminder that the estimates aggregate
many small within-firm contrasts rather than a few large ones. A collapsed panel at the HS6--destination--year level (3.77
million cells; outcome: within-cell mean of log exports; regressions weighted by cell
size; fixed effects $pd+dt+pt$), in the gravity-equation tradition of
\citet{headmayer2014}, reproduces the design at a fraction of the cost, with $dt$ playing
the role that $fdt$ plays in the full panel: it absorbs the agreement.

What it does not reproduce is the firm dimension, and the two panels should be compared
with that in mind rather than treated as interchangeable. Re-estimating the full panel
with $pd+dt+pt$ in place of $fpd+fdt+pt$ recovers the collapsed coefficients to seven
significant figures ($-0.0045685$ vs.\ $-0.0045685$ on green; $-0.011873$ vs.\
$-0.011873$ on dirty), which establishes that the weighted collapse is algebraically the
micro-regression under those fixed effects and that the two pipelines agree exactly where
they are asked the same question. It also isolates what the firm effects do. On the green
margin the two structures agree substantively ($-0.0046$ collapsed, $-0.0023$ full panel;
both indistinguishable from zero). On the dirty margin they differ by a factor of
$2.7$ ($-0.0119$ vs.\ $-0.0044$), and the gap is a result rather than a discrepancy: the
collapsed specification compares products \emph{across} the firms serving a
destination--year, so any shift in which firms export dirty goods enters the coefficient;
$fdt$ removes that channel and leaves only reallocation within the firm. Roughly
three-fifths of the collapsed dirty coefficient is therefore between-firm composition, not
within-firm response --- consistent with Section~\ref{sec:dirty}'s reading, and a reason
the full panel is the reference specification.

\subsection{Inference with few treated clusters}

There are 225 destination clusters in the full-panel reference specification (236 in
the collapsed panel) but only 23 treated ones, and effectively 14
agreements.\footnote{Every estimate in the paper was produced twice, in \textsc{Stata}
(\texttt{reghdfe}, \texttt{boottest}, \texttt{ppmlhdfe}, \texttt{eventstudyinteract}) and
in \textsc{R} (\texttt{fixest}, \texttt{fwildclusterboot}). Point estimates agree to at
least eight significant digits throughout. The numbers reported are the \textsc{Stata}
ones, because the do-files are the more direct route to replication. Two quantities
legitimately differ between implementations and are discussed where they arise:
bootstrap and permutation $p$-values, which carry Monte Carlo error, and the
Sun--Abraham standard errors of Appendix~\ref{app:sunab}.} Asymptotic cluster-robust inference is unreliable in this regime, so every
headline estimate is subjected to three tests: (i) destination-clustered asymptotics;
(ii) wild cluster bootstrap with 9{,}999 replications \citep{cameron2008,
fischer2021, roodman2019} --- computed on Frisch--Waugh-residualized data (fixed effects
demeaned once, not re-estimated at each bootstrap draw). This carries two approximations,
both in the collapsed-panel implementation only. It treats $pt$ as if it were nested
within the destination cluster, which it is not, while $pd$ and $dt$ are. And the
small-sample corrections see the four regressors of the residualized equation rather than
the four plus the absorbed fixed effects, so the degrees-of-freedom adjustment is not
exact. Demeaned coefficients match the unrestricted \texttt{fixest} estimates exactly, so
the point estimates are unaffected. Neither approximation applies to the full-panel
bootstrap, which runs natively after \texttt{reghdfe} and is the version quoted for the
headline intervals in Section~\ref{sec:results}. With only 23 \emph{treated} clusters among 236, the more demanding regime of
\citet{mackinnon2017} and \citet{conley2011} applies: standard wild bootstrap asymptotics
assume the number of treated clusters, not just clusters overall, grows large, which is
why (iii) permutation inference \citep{fisher1935} is run as an independent check rather
than a formality. Entire EP profiles
(depth and timing) are randomly reassigned 1{,}000 times across treated destinations,
asking whether the observed coefficient stands out among placebos --- the sharpest
answer to ``is it really the environmental content, or would any labelling of these
destinations produce this number?''. Three features of the test should be stated rather
than left implicit. The null is the restricted one --- which treated destination holds
which profile --- not treated versus untreated, since never-treated destinations are
never given a profile. Depth and timing are permuted jointly, so the test asks about the
whole treatment assignment and cannot separate environmental content from the date of
entry. And the effective support is far smaller than 1{,}000: the eleven ASEAN
destinations carry identical profiles, so permuting them among themselves changes
nothing, leaving roughly nine distinct profiles. The permutation distribution is
correspondingly granular, and the resolution of the resulting $p$-values is bounded by
that, not by the number of draws. The dynamic specification is an event study of the
green (dirty) vs.\ neutral differential around entry into force, with never-treated
destinations as controls, endpoint bins declared, and a Sun--Abraham version
\citep{sun2021} run on the destination-level composition gap to address staggered-timing
cohort heterogeneity \citep{goodmanbacon2021, dechaisemartin2020, callaway2021}.

\subsection{The green margin: a stable, precise null}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the main estimates. In the full firm-level panel the
EP$\times$green coefficient is $-0.0022$ (s.e.\ 0.0039) with the WB index and $-0.0001$
(s.e.\ 0.0010) with TREND; the joint F-test on all four composition interactions is
$p=0.31$ and $p=0.71$ respectively. The collapsed panel returns $-0.0046$ (WB): the wild
cluster bootstrap p-value is 0.65, and permutation inference on the exact estimating
equation --- 1{,}000 reassignments of entire EP profiles across treated destinations
--- gives $p=0.60$: the observed coefficient is well within the bulk of the placebo
distribution.

To gauge magnitudes, it helps to benchmark the confidence intervals against the
literature's reference estimate (Table~\ref{tab:brandi}). \citet{brandi2020} find that one \emph{liberal}
environmental provision raises the green share of developing-country exports by 0.4
percentage points, about $+17\%$ of the mean green share --- roughly $+0.16$ log points
of green relative to non-green exports per provision in the metric of
equation~\eqref{eq:main}. Section~\ref{sec:strategy} argued that asymptotic inference is
unreliable with 23 treated clusters; that argument applies to interval estimates as much
as to p-values, so the bounds quoted here are wild cluster bootstrap intervals. The
full-panel 95\% bootstrap confidence interval for EP$\times$green is
$[-0.0353, +0.0355]$ per WB provision (collapsed panel: $[-0.0182, +0.0317]$). The
corresponding asymptotic interval, $[-0.0100, +0.0055]$, is roughly six times narrower,
and reporting precision on that basis would claim exactly the kind of certainty this
paper's own inference section declares unwarranted. On the bootstrap bound, the upper
limit is about one quarter of the Brandi-equivalent effect: the design rules out their
point estimate and anything above roughly a quarter of it, and is uninformative below that
threshold. This is a weaker statement than the asymptotic interval would support, and it
is the one the design can sustain. On
the dirty margin their trade-restrictive provisions reduce dirty exports by roughly 5\%
of the mean per provision ($\approx -0.05$ log points); even taking our asymptotic
full-panel point estimate at face value ($-0.0044$ per provision), the response is an
order of magnitude smaller. Both comparisons carry the same caveat in both directions:
Brandi et al.'s effects are identified from explicitly liberal or trade-restrictive
clauses, which are almost entirely absent from Chinese agreements of this period
(Section~\ref{sec:robust}) --- which is precisely the interpretation the paper
advances. In per-standard-deviation terms, a one-SD increase in WB EP depth in the
estimating sample ($\approx$ 2.383 provisions, weighted by cell size and inclusive of
never-treated destination--years at EP=0) moves green relative to neutral exports by
at most $-8.4\%$ to $+8.5\%$ at the 95\% bootstrap bounds ($-2.4\%$ to $+1.3\%$ on
asymptotic bounds).

\subsection{Dynamics}

Figure~\ref{fig:es} plots the event study. Differential pre-trends are flat for both
green and dirty products, and nothing happens at entry into force. This is a
falsification check, not a proof: parallel trends is a statement about the unobserved
counterfactual path of treated destinations after treatment, which no pre-period test
can directly verify; a flat pre-trend rules out the most detectable violations (an
already-diverging composition gap) without establishing that the assumption holds
exactly thereafter. The late negative drift for green products (bin
$\geq$+5) is identified only by early cohorts (ASEAN 2005, Chile 2006, Pakistan 2007,
New Zealand 2008) and does not survive the Sun--Abraham estimator applied to the
destination-level composition gap: the aggregated ATT is $-0.042$ ($p=0.27$) for the
green gap and $+0.073$ ($p=0.28$) for the dirty gap --- consistent with cohort
heterogeneity rather than a treatment effect \citep{sun2021}. That estimator answers a
narrower question than equation~\eqref{eq:main} and should not be read as confirming it:
it binarizes treatment (EP present versus absent, discarding depth), carries no depth
control, and runs on a destination--year composition gap built by collapsing across
products, so it has no product--year fixed effects. It is a diagnostic for staggered
timing and cohort heterogeneity --- which is what it is used for here --- not an
independent replication of the static specification. The full Sun--Abraham
event study is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:sunab}: no lead or lag in the
$[-10,+8]$ window is individually distinguishable from zero, so the interaction-weighted
estimator agrees with the two-way fixed-effects picture on both the absence of a
pre-trend and the absence of a break at entry.

\subsection{The dirty margin: anatomy of a false positive}\label{sec:dirty}

The strongly significant WB$\times$dirty coefficient in the collapsed panel
($-0.0119$, asymptotic $p<0.001$) deserves its own subsection, because it is a
textbook illustration of why few-cluster inference matters: every robust check the
coefficient is put through returns it to the null. The wild cluster bootstrap raises its
p-value to 0.07, just above the conventional 5\% threshold. Permutation inference on the
exact estimating equation agrees: reshuffling entire EP profiles across treated
destinations, 27.7\% of placebo draws produce a coefficient as large in absolute value
($p=0.28$), placing the observed estimate squarely inside the placebo distribution. A
coarser permutation on a destination--year--product-type aggregate reverses the sign
entirely (+0.005, $p=0.49$): aggregating to one observation per
destination--year--product-type collapses the within-cell heterogeneity across firms and
product varieties that the disaggregated coefficient exploits, so the two permutation
tests are not answering quite the same question --- but they reach the same verdict, and
the sign instability under aggregation is further evidence of fragility. A leave-one-out
exercise across the 23 treated destinations points the same way: the coefficient is
stable ($\approx -0.012$) except when Australia is dropped, in which case it falls to
$-0.0103$ with $p=0.24$. South Korea, the destination that drove the equivalent result in
an earlier version of this analysis, is no longer pivotal on its own: dropping it leaves
the coefficient at $-0.0097$ with $p=0.09$. The TREND index
never shows the effect (bootstrap $p=0.86$). In
the full firm-level panel the coefficient is $-0.0044$ with an asymptotic $p$ of 0.052
--- just above conventional significance, the same order of magnitude as in the CEM
subsample --- and the wild cluster bootstrap on that same panel moves it to $p=0.18$,
with a 95\% interval of $[-0.043, +0.011]$ that comfortably contains zero. The marginal
asymptotic signal is thus erased by robust inference on both panels independently, not
only on the collapsed one. The honest reading is that the dirty margin is a false positive of exactly the
kind few-cluster designs manufacture: a coefficient that is overwhelming under asymptotic
standard errors ($p<0.001$) and that no form of robust inference sustains. It belongs in
the paper as a descriptive pattern and a methodological warning, not as an identified
effect. Section~\ref{sec:robust} shows this is compounded by the near-total absence of
trade-restrictive content in Chinese agreements' dirty-relevant chapters, the same
structural limitation that governs the green margin.

\section{Robustness and extensions}\label{sec:robust}

\subsection{Provision bundling and the limits of heterogeneity analysis}

A natural objection is that the aggregate index dilutes provisions with a trade
mechanism (green-goods liberalization; binding standards) with cooperation language that
has none. The objection is correct in principle --- and unanswerable in this setting,
which is itself a finding. The imbalance can be quantified directly, and it is large in
both codings. Summing WB\_EP\_Depth across the 25 treated destinations gives 150
provisions checked in total; only 5.3\% of them fall in \texttt{GreenLiberalization} or
\texttt{StandardsNonRegression}, the two sub-indices with a direct trade mechanism.
TREND tells the same story on an independently coded, far more granular scheme: of 437
provisions checked across the same destinations, \texttt{GreenMarketAccess} accounts for
0.92\%. At the agreement level, TREND's own binding/non-binding distinction
(\texttt{X5.01.01 Binding obligations}) is coded present in exactly one of the 14--15
Chinese agreements in the database (China--Korea, 2015); every other agreement scores
zero on that specific clause. Table~\ref{tab:mechanism-share} summarizes both codings
side by side. What follows is a mechanism argument \emph{for the null}, not
evidence of a positive causal channel: it explains why no effect is detected, rather
than tracing how an effect would operate if one existed. Across the 223 treated country--year observations in the
estimation sample, the two WB sub-indices with a direct mechanism
(\texttt{GreenLiberalization} and \texttt{StandardsNonRegression}) are \emph{perfectly}
collinear ($\rho = 1.000$) --- and the reason is stark: both are non-zero in only three
country--years (Korea from 2015; Switzerland from 2014), always in the same 1:3
proportion. Mechanism-bearing provisions effectively exist in two Chinese agreements of
the period. Their interactions load identically in the regression (identical
t-statistics; coefficients in exact 3:1 ratio). TREND
sub-indices correlate between 0.5 and 0.9 with each other and with the total count.
With $\sim$14 agreements, provision-type heterogeneity is not separately identifiable
--- in sharp contrast with \citet{brandi2020}, whose 680-agreement panel allows it (they
report a maximum VIF of 4.6 across their provision-type variables; here, for the two
mechanism-bearing sub-indices, it is unbounded). One reading rule applies to everything
that follows: each sub-index is estimated \emph{on its own}, replacing the aggregate index
one at a time, not entered jointly. The coefficients are therefore not partial effects
holding other clause types constant --- each absorbs whatever variation in the correlated
sub-indices moves with it. Entering them jointly is precisely what the collinearity
documented above rules out, so this is the only feasible reading, but it means the table
is a set of separate comparisons and not a decomposition of the aggregate index into
additive parts. What can be said: the
mechanism-bearing bundle shows the same pattern as the aggregate index --- green never
responds (green-liberalization $\times$ green: $p=0.90$; TREND green-market-access
$\times$ green: $p=0.38$), while the dirty interaction is marginally negative on
asymptotic p-values ($p=0.04$--$0.07$), the same asymptotics-only signal as the
aggregate --- which Section~\ref{sec:dirty} shows does not survive robust inference. The
two placebo components with no trade mechanism behave differently, and the difference is
worth stating plainly rather than averaging away. TREND soft provisions show nothing on
either margin ($\times$ green: $p=0.27$; $\times$ dirty: $p=0.86$), as the selection
logic would predict. Regulatory-space clauses do not: they carry $+0.024$ on green and
$+0.023$ on dirty, and both survive the wild cluster bootstrap ($p=0.046$ and $p=0.022$
respectively, B=9{,}999). This is the only place in the paper where a component with no
trade mechanism registers a signal that robust inference sustains, and it is reported
here as a caveat, not resolved. Two features bound what can be read into it. First, the
two margins move together: the green--dirty differential is $+0.0017$ with $p=0.80$, so
the estimate describes green and dirty exports rising by the same amount against the
neutral baseline --- a pattern that is not the signature of a shift in composition toward
cleaner goods, which is the object this paper measures. Second, and more fundamentally,
this sub-index is not a clean placebo to begin with. Regulatory-space clauses account for
71.5\% of the TREND count across treated destination--years and correlate 0.90 with the
TotalDepth control; in this specification the depth control itself flips to significantly
negative ($\times$ green: $-0.0011$, $p=0.006$). Two regressors correlated at 0.90
splitting into a large positive and a large negative coefficient is this section's
bundling problem applied to the placebo, not an independent channel measured cleanly. The
defensible conclusion is the weaker one: this design cannot separate a regulatory-space
effect from general agreement depth, which means it cannot use that sub-index as a clean
falsification test either. Enforcement provisions --- dispute-settlement
mechanisms attached to the environmental chapter, which condition whether any of the
above is binding rather than providing a trade mechanism themselves --- are likewise
null on both margins and both codings (WB \texttt{EnforcementDSM} $\times$ green:
$p=0.91$; $\times$ dirty: $p=0.90$; TREND \texttt{EnforcementDSM} $\times$ green:
$p=0.78$; $\times$ dirty: $p=0.71$).

This bundling result also reconciles the null with \citet{brandi2020}'s positive finding,
even though both papers draw on the same underlying TREND/World Bank provision coding.
Their effect is not a generic ``environmental provisions matter'' result: it is driven
specifically by the subset of agreements containing explicitly trade-liberalizing or
trade-restrictive environmental clauses, identified separately from cooperation-only
language because their 680-agreement panel has enough cross-agreement variation in
provision \emph{type} to support that decomposition (maximum VIF 4.6 across
provision-type variables). The Chinese PTAs of 2000--2015 are, on this same coding
scheme, overwhelmingly the cooperation-only type: mechanism-bearing content --- the exact
category driving Brandi et al.'s result --- appears in only 2 of the 14 agreements, and
is perfectly collinear across its two components in-sample. With that little variation in
provision type, this design cannot separately identify a mechanism-bearing effect even if
one exists, and a null on the aggregate index is what Brandi et al.'s own heterogeneity
would predict for a sample built almost entirely from cooperation-only chapters. The two
results are therefore complementary rather than contradictory: theirs shows that
mechanism-bearing provisions move trade when agreements contain them in sufficient
number and variety; this paper shows that China's agreements, over this period, did not
contain them.

A second, compounding difference is the level of aggregation at which the two designs
operate. \citet{brandi2020} work with UN Comtrade flows aggregated to the
exporter--importer--year level, with dirty/green export shares as the outcome; a
composition effect there can arise simply because the set of firms or products exporting
changes, without any single firm reallocating its own mix. The data used here are
firm--product(HS6)--destination--year transactions, and the preferred specification
saturates firm$\times$destination$\times$year fixed effects, so the EP coefficients are
identified from within-firm reallocation across products at a given destination and year
--- a strictly more demanding test of the same composition hypothesis, and one on which a
country-pair-level aggregation could in principle mask heterogeneity that this design is
built to detect.

\subsection{Extensive margin}

Log-linear estimates condition on positive flows. If EPs created green trade at the
extensive margin --- combinations with no recorded trade before --- the intensive
estimates would miss it \citep{santossilva2006, larch2025}. The test available here
operates at the product--destination level, not the firm level: on a zero-filled
HS6--destination--year grid (8.2 million cells), it detects green products newly exported
to a destination, but not a new firm beginning to export a product that other Chinese
firms already ship there --- which is the more common form of entry. The extensive margin
is therefore probed, not exhausted. PPML estimates of
equation~\eqref{eq:main}'s aggregated analogue confirm the null: EP$\times$green is
$+0.0015$ ($p=0.74$) with the WB index and $+0.0001$ ($p=0.95$) with TREND;
EP$\times$dirty is $-0.030$ ($p=0.06$) and $+0.003$ ($p=0.55$) respectively. There is
no green trade creation at the extensive margin, and the dirty-margin coefficient here
is, if anything, marginally stronger than in the intensive-margin specifications ---
consistent with the false-positive reading of Section~\ref{sec:dirty}: it does not clear
the conventional 5\% threshold on this grid either.

\subsection{Within-firm reallocation: a descriptive complement}

The margin that aggregate data cannot see: does the green \emph{share} of a
multiproduct firm's basket toward a destination respond to EP depth? On the
firm--destination--year panel of green shares (13.3 million observations; FE:
firm--destination and year; destination-clustered s.e.), the coefficient on WB EP
depth is $-0.0001$ ($p=0.50$) and on the TREND count $-0.00006$ ($p=0.043$,
asymptotic). Both are economically negligible: a one-standard-deviation increase in
the TREND count moves the within-firm green share by three hundredths of a percentage
point, with the ``wrong'' sign, and the marginal asymptotic significance is subject to
the same few-clusters caveat as everything else in this setting.

This module is reported as a descriptive complement, not as identifying evidence, and
the reason is the one given in Section~\ref{sec:strategy}. The specification regresses
the green share on the \emph{level} of EP depth with firm--destination and year fixed
effects; it does not include the firm--destination--year absorption that the triple
difference relies on, and it therefore inherits precisely the confound that
Section~\ref{sec:strategy} argues is fatal to any level specification here --- EP depth
switching on with the agreement, and ``environmental depth'' being indistinguishable
from ``having a deep agreement'' across $\sim$14 agreements. A null recovered from a
design that cannot identify an effect is not evidence that no effect exists; it is
uninformative in both directions, and the marginally significant TREND coefficient
illustrates the point rather than undercutting it. What the module does establish is
descriptive and worth having: the within-firm green share is flat with respect to EP
depth in the raw conditional means, which is at least consistent with the identified
composition null and would have been a puzzle had it pointed the other way. It is not
a firm-level counterpart to the within-plant evidence of \citet{cherniwchan2017}, whose
NAFTA tariff variation identifies a level effect that this setting cannot; establishing
that comparison would require re-specifying the share equation as a triple difference
with firm--destination--year effects, which the current design leaves for future work.

\subsection{How much the depth control decides}

Environmental depth and general agreement depth move together at $0.96$ within
destination and year, and any regression containing both must split their shared
variation between them. How it splits depends on which depth measure is used, so a single
coefficient understates the real uncertainty. Table~\ref{tab:depthbounds} therefore
reports the green coefficient under four depth controls rather than one: none at all, the
aggregate WB \texttt{TotalDepth}, a targeted version restricted to the 14 non-environmental
areas that actually correlate with EP content, and the externally-coded DESTA index
\citep{dur2014}.

\subsection{Environmental share of agreement content}

The main specification measures the marginal effect of one more environmental provision.
A different question --- the one \citet{abman2024} pose --- is whether, \emph{given} that
an agreement exists, a more environmentally-weighted agreement moves composition. That
contrast requires restricting to PTA partners and replacing the level of EP depth with
its share of total depth, $EP_{dt}/TD_{dt}$. On the collapsed panel restricted to the 23
partner destinations it contains (Hong Kong and Macao being excluded throughout;
534{,}846 cells, of which 516{,}684 survive iterative singleton
removal; the share takes 12 distinct values in
$[0.012, 0.068]$), the green interaction is $-2.25$ (s.e.\ 1.15, $p=0.06$) and the dirty
interaction $-1.60$ (s.e.\ 1.55, $p=0.32$). Two cautions apply, and together they keep
this from being read as a result. The estimand changes: this is the effect of the
environmental \emph{composition} of an agreement, not of an additional clause, so the
coefficients are not on a scale comparable with the rest of the paper. And the share
varies far less than the level does (coefficient of variation 0.19 against 0.62 on the
same destinations), so the standard errors are correspondingly wide and the green
coefficient clears neither conventional threshold despite its size. It is reported
because the contrast is the one the closest antecedent in the literature uses, and
because a marginally negative green coefficient is the opposite of what a green
trade-creation story predicts --- not because this design identifies it well.

\subsection{Sample robustness}

Table~\ref{tab:robust} collects the full-panel sample variations. Adding controls (MFN
tariff, market concentration, antidumping exposure) moves the green coefficient to an
exact zero ($-0.0002$, $p=0.94$). Excluding the eleven ASEAN destinations --- the block
that dominates the treated count, and whose single 2005 accession date means the
pooled coefficient could in principle be answering an ASEAN-specific rather than a
general question --- leaves it at $-0.0025$ ($p=0.48$): the estimate is not an artifact
of how heavily that one bloc weights the aggregation. Re-including
Hong Kong and Macao gives $-0.0009$ ($p=0.80$). Restricting to common-support products
gives $-0.0022$ ($p=0.57$). Across every variation the dirty coefficient sits between
$-0.0040$ and $-0.0060$ with asymptotic p-values between 0.02 and 0.09 --- persistently
marginal, and, as Section~\ref{sec:dirty} established, robust to none of the bootstrap,
permutation, or leave-one-out checks.

A remaining caveat concerns the tariff control, and it is worth stating with the
provenance made explicit. The variable is the applied duty recorded in the customs data
rather than a rate constructed here; it varies across destinations for the same
HS6--year in 97.9\% of product--year cells, so it is a destination-level tariff facing
Chinese exporters and not a Chinese import duty, and its mean does not fall after PTA
entry for partner destinations, so it is the MFN rather than the bilateral preferential
rate. The preferential rate was not obtainable (the WITS-TRAINS API was unavailable at
the time of writing). Three considerations bound what this costs. The tariff does not
enter equation~\eqref{eq:main} at all --- it appears only in the ``with controls'' row of
Table~\ref{tab:robust} and in the CEM matching --- so the headline estimates do not
depend on it. Whatever tariff liberalization the agreements delivered varies at the
destination--year level and is absorbed by $\theta_{fdt}$, and at the product level by
$\theta_{pt}$. And the residual threat runs in a conservative direction: if PTA tariff
cuts had been deeper on green goods than on neutral ones, the omitted preferential
margin would load onto EP$\times$green with a positive sign, inflating rather than
suppressing the estimate. The estimate is zero.

\subsection{Destination-specific trends in the composition gap}

The conservative-bias argument of Section~\ref{sec:strategy} has a testable
counterpart, and it is the paper's central falsification exercise: if destinations
signing deep environmental chapters have green demand rising for reasons of their own
--- a rival explanation for the null result, not merely a robustness formality ---
allowing each destination its own linear trend in the green (and dirty) gap, estimated
from data the treatment cannot have contaminated, should matter. Two variants of this exercise, run on the
collapsed panel, bracket the answer. The first adds destination-specific linear trends
interacted with green and dirty status, estimated over the full sample period. The WB
results are essentially unchanged (EP$\times$green $-0.0051$, bootstrap $p=0.50$;
EP$\times$dirty $-0.0082$, bootstrap $p=0.28$), but the TREND green interaction flips
sign relative to the baseline (from $+0.0018$, $p=0.32$, to $-0.0022$) and --- the only
coefficient in the paper's destination-trend and robustness battery to do so ---
survives the wild cluster bootstrap ($p=0.012$). Taken at face value it would say
environmental provisions \emph{reduce} green exports. It should not be taken at face
value: unit-specific trends estimated over the full sample are known to absorb
post-treatment dynamics and can reverse the sign of treatment coefficients when
effects or cohort dynamics are present \citep{wolfers2006}, and the negative late
drift of the green gap that these trends pick up is precisely the early-cohort
artifact that the Sun--Abraham estimator already dismissed
(Appendix~\ref{app:sunab}). The second variant removes the contamination by
construction: destination-specific trends are estimated on \emph{pre-treatment} years
only (all years for never-treated destinations) and projected forward before
re-estimating the main specification on the detrended outcome. Every coefficient
returns to an imprecise zero. (That variant estimates destination slopes in a first
stage and subtracts them before re-estimating, so the second-stage inference does not
carry the first-stage estimation error; with intervals this wide the omission is formal
rather than material, but it is one more reason to read the exercise as diagnostic.)
The coefficients are TREND$\times$green $+0.0074$, bootstrap $p=0.18$;
WB$\times$green $+0.0168$, bootstrap $p=0.71$; dirty interactions $p=0.30$--$0.62$).
That the one nominally robust coefficient in the trend specification reverses sign
entirely depending on how the trends are estimated identifies it as an artifact of the
trend specification, not a treatment effect; the composition null stands against
destination-specific linear drifts in green demand, the most plausible form of the
selection-on-trends confounder.

\subsection{A continuous dirty margin: CO\textsubscript{2} intensity}

The binary dirty indicator groups HS6 products into the six classic
pollution-intensive sectors of \citet{mani1998}; a natural concern is that a
continuous, externally sourced measure of pollution intensity might tell a different
story. I construct one from the replication data of \citet{shapiro2021}, which
reports China-specific CO\textsubscript{2} emission intensity (direct and total,
i.e.\ including upstream inputs) for 47 EXIOBASE industries. Each industry code
encodes a two-digit ISIC Rev.\ 3 / NACE Rev.\ 1.1 division (verified against the
subset of industries the replication package labels by name: e.g.\ \emph{Pulp}
loads on division 21, \emph{Coke oven products} on division 23, \emph{Cement, lime
and plaster} on division 26, \emph{Iron and steel} and \emph{Aluminum} on division
27 --- exactly the ISIC divisions underlying the six Mani-Wheeler sectors). HS6
products are mapped to ISIC3 divisions via the WITS HS1996--ISIC3 concordance
already used for the binary classification, and assigned the simple average
China-specific intensity across the EXIOBASE sub-industries in their division (no
sub-industry weights are available in the replication data); unmatched HS6 codes
(9.5\% of the panel) are assigned the sample mean. The resulting measure validates
against the existing trichotomy without circularity, since it is built independently
of the green/dirty/neutral classification: mean intensity is $0.0012$ for dirty
products, $0.0008$ for neutral products, and $0.0006$ for green products ---
monotonic in the expected direction.

Re-estimating equation~\eqref{eq:main} on the collapsed panel with EP interacted with
the standardized continuous intensity in place of the binary dirty indicator gives
the same reading as the rest of the paper: EP$\times$intensity is $-0.0025$ with the
WB index (bootstrap $p=0.71$) and $-0.0013$ with TREND (asymptotic $p=0.012$,
bootstrap $p=0.06$) --- a fade under robust inference consistent with the marginal,
non-robust pattern documented throughout Section~\ref{sec:dirty}, not a new or
sharper result. The continuous measure does not overturn the conclusion that the
composition null is not an artifact of how dirty products are classified.

\subsection{Outcome trimming}

The baseline conditions on all positive flows without trimming; the design-based
inference battery is the primary guard against influential observations. As a
moment-based complement, I trim log export value at its 1st and 99th percentiles.
Trimming removes 2.0\% of collapsed-panel cells (3,773,498 to 3,698,033); after the
usual iterative singleton removal the estimation sample is 3,605,798 cells. The full
panel retains 44.8 million observations.

Point estimates are virtually unchanged. On the collapsed panel, EP$\times$green
is $-0.0048$ (WB, bootstrap $p=0.61$) and $+0.0018$ (TREND, bootstrap $p=0.41$);
EP$\times$dirty is $-0.01159$ (WB, bootstrap $p=0.041$) and $+0.00025$ (TREND,
bootstrap $p=0.89$). On the full panel (44.8 million observations), the WB dirty coefficient is
$-0.01170$ (bootstrap $p=0.066$, $B=9{,}999$, Frisch--Waugh decomposition
estimated in \textsc{Stata}/\texttt{reghdfe} + \texttt{boottest}); the TREND
coefficient is $+0.00027$ (bootstrap $p=0.90$, $B=9{,}999$). The green null is
unaffected. The dirty margin reproduces the same pattern as the baseline ---
marginally significant under one coding, null under the other, and not robust
across both --- ruling out outlier influence as an alternative explanation.

\subsection{Export value decomposition: quantity and unit value}

Log export value conflates shipment volume and price per unit. A reallocation
toward cleaner products could in principle operate through either channel, and two
effects of opposite sign could cancel in the aggregate outcome. To rule out this
masking, I re-estimate equation~\eqref{eq:main} on the collapsed panel replacing
the outcome with log export quantity and log export unit value (value divided by
quantity, both in logs). Both outcomes are constructed at the HS6--destination--year
cell and estimated with the same FE structure and wild cluster bootstrap used
throughout ($B=9{,}999$; layer-2 cross-check against \textsc{Stata}/\texttt{reghdfe}).

Neither channel registers a significant effect, for either index or either product
type. Quantity margin: EP$\times$green $-0.0055$ (WB, $p=0.53$), $+0.0019$
(TREND, $p=0.43$); EP$\times$dirty $-0.0115$ (WB, $p=0.25$), $-0.0004$
(TREND, $p=0.89$). Unit-value margin: EP$\times$green $+0.0005$ (WB, $p=0.85$),
$-0.0001$ (TREND, $p=0.87$); EP$\times$dirty $+0.0006$ (WB, $p=0.95$),
$+0.0009$ (TREND, $p=0.16$). The composition null is not an averaging artifact:
EP depth moves neither the volume nor the price of green or dirty exports relative
to the neutral baseline.

% ============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================================

Using the universe of Chinese customs transactions and two independent codings of the
environmental content of China's trade agreements, this paper finds that environmental
provisions did not change what China exports, on the green margin or at the extensive
margin. The green result is a \emph{bounded null}: stable across estimation designs and
sample variations, robust to an inference battery built for the few-treated-clusters
regime that characterizes this --- and most --- PTA settings, with bootstrap bounds that
rule out effects larger than about one quarter of the benchmark estimate in the aggregate
literature and say nothing below that threshold. A descriptive within-firm share panel
points the same way, though the level specification it requires is not separately
identified here (Section~\ref{sec:robust}). The dirty margin is the one apparent
exception, and it is a \emph{non-result} rather than a second bounded null: a negative
coefficient that is overwhelming under asymptotic standard errors, clears none of the same
battery, and is carried by a single destination (Section~\ref{sec:dirty}) --- a false
positive of the kind few-treated-cluster designs manufacture, reported here as a
descriptive pattern and a cautionary case rather than an identified effect.

The reading is not that environmental provisions never matter: \citet{abman2024} show
they can, on environmental outcomes, when they are specific and enforceable. The reading
is that the environmental chapters China signed in 2000--2015 --- thin, bundled, and
dominated by cooperation language --- had no trade bite. For the policy debate on
greening trade agreements, the distinction matters: content design, not chapter
presence, is what could move flows. For the empirical literature, the paper adds a
cautionary methodological tale: with treatment varying across a dozen agreements,
asymptotically significant composition effects appear and dissolve under honest
inference; the discipline of bootstrap, permutation, and leave-one-out should be
standard equipment.

\section{Sun--Abraham dynamics on the composition gap}\label{app:sunab}
% ============================================================================

Figure~\ref{fig:sunab} plots the interaction-weighted event study of
\citet{sun2021} estimated on the destination--year composition gaps (the difference
between the weighted mean outcome of green, respectively dirty, products and that of
neutral products within each destination--year), with never-treated destinations as
the control group and $t=-1$ as the reference period. The aggregated ATTs confirm the
static results: $-0.042$ ($p=0.27$) for the green gap and $+0.073$ ($p=0.28$) for the
dirty gap.

The standard errors reported here account for the fact that the cohort shares used to
aggregate the cohort-specific effects are \emph{estimated} rather than known, as
\citet{sun2021} prescribe. This is not a technicality: the two implementations of the
estimator used in this project agree on every coefficient to fifteen digits but not on
their precision, because one of them treats the shares as fixed weights. The difference
is zero exactly where a single cohort identifies the period --- there is nothing to
estimate --- and grows to a factor of three to four at the distant leads, where many
cohorts with sharply discordant estimates are averaged. Since the pre-period leads are
precisely where cohort dispersion is largest, treating the weights as known would
manufacture apparent pre-trends out of that dispersion.\footnote{Concretely, the dirty
gap at $t=-6$ is $+0.047$ with a standard error of $0.014$ if the shares are treated as
known ($p=0.001$) and of $0.048$ once their estimation is priced in ($p=0.34$). The
cohort-specific estimates behind that number run from $-0.49$ (2006, Chile) and $-0.33$
(2010, Peru) to $+0.14$ (2008, New Zealand) and $+0.10$ (2015, Australia): there is no
common displacement of dirty exports ahead of the agreements, only destination-specific
fluctuations of opposite sign, and the correct variance says so. Consistently, dropping
the 2015 cohort flips the coefficient to $-0.051$ and dropping the 2014--15 cohorts to
$-0.109$ --- a genuine pre-trend does not change sign when one destination is removed.
The two-way fixed-effects event study of Figure~\ref{fig:es} shows nothing at $t=-6$
either ($+0.027$, $p=0.53$).}

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/eventstudy_sunab.png}
\caption{Sun--Abraham event study on the destination--year composition gap, window
$[-6,+5]$; destination and year fixed effects, cell-count weights,

With those standard errors, no lead and no lag in the $[-10,+8]$ window is individually
distinguishable from zero on either margin: the pre-period is flat, and nothing happens
at entry into force. Two distant leads on the dirty gap ($t=-14$ and $t=-12$) and one
distant lag on the green gap ($t=+10$) do clear the 5\% threshold, but they lie outside
the window plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:sunab} and each rests on one or two destinations
observed that far from their entry date; with 28 lead and lag coefficients estimated on
23 treated clusters, two or three nominally significant ones are what sampling noise
delivers under exactly flat pre-trends.

The aggregated ATT --- the statistic with a valid interpretation under staggered
timing --- is null in every variant: $p=0.28$ in the baseline, $p=0.11$ restricting to
the $[-6,+5]$ window, $p=0.30$ excluding the 2014--15 cohorts. This is the sense in
