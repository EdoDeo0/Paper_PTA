# The Persistence of Early Childhood Maturity: International Evidence of Long-Run Age Effects

**Bedard & Dhuey (2006)**
*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, Vol. 121, No. 4, pp. 1437–1472
[Paper Link](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/121/4/1437/1914586)

---

## Abstract

A continuum of ages exists at school entry due to the use of a single school cutoff date — making the "oldest" children approximately 20 percent older than the "youngest" children. We provide substantial evidence that these initial maturity differences have long-lasting effects on student performance across OECD countries. In particular, the youngest members of each cohort score 4–12 percentiles lower than the oldest members in grade four and 2–9 percentiles lower in grade eight. In fact, data from Canada and the United States show that the youngest members of each cohort are even less likely to attend university.

---

## Research Question

Do initial age-based maturity differences generated by a single annual school cutoff date persist into adolescence and affect long-run educational outcomes — and is this pattern common across countries with very different education systems? The source of variation is month of birth relative to the national school cutoff date (assigned relative age), which is treated as exogenous since birth timing is not strategically manipulated around the cutoff. The primary estimator is 2SLS/IV, with assigned relative age instrumenting for observed age; the reduced form is also emphasized as the key policy-relevant parameter.

---

## Results

Relative age effects at the fourth grade level are large and pervasive: the oldest cohort members score 4–12 percentiles higher than the youngest in math and science across all OECD countries studied, with point estimates especially large in "clean" countries with essentially no grade retention (England, Iceland, Japan, Norway: 11–12 percentile gaps). Effects persist into grade eight: the oldest score 2–9 percentiles higher in 14 of 19 countries. OLS estimates are systematically downward biased — and sometimes negative — because retained students are disproportionately old but low-scoring. The only countries without detectable effects at grade eight are Denmark and Finland, where formal curriculum-based education begins late and ability grouping is restricted. Long-run effects are confirmed: in British Columbia the relatively oldest students are 9.8% more likely (reduced form) to be in a pre-university program; in the United States they are 7.7% more likely to take the SAT/ACT and 11.6% more likely to enroll in an accredited four-year college.

---

## Data

The paper draws on five data sources across multiple countries and two supplementary national datasets for long-run outcomes.

**Primary source — TIMSS:** The 1995 and 1999 Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study provide nationally representative math and science test scores for third/fourth graders (1995) and seventh/eighth graders (1995 and 1999) across OECD countries. The authors restrict the sample to OECD countries with unambiguous nationwide school entry cutoff dates, excluding Australia, Germany, Hungary, Ireland, the Netherlands, Switzerland, and the United States (from TIMSS) because their cutoff rules vary by region or are discretionary, and excluding Korea and Turkey for data quality concerns. This leaves 10 countries for grades 3–4 and 18 countries for grades 7–8 (19 countries total), for a raw sample of 228,629 students, reduced to 225,772 after dropping observations with missing sex, birth month/year, test year, or test month. Scores are standardized to mean 50, standard deviation 10, first within country by test booklet and then internationally across all TIMSS participants. School cutoff dates are identified empirically from the modal birth-month cluster in each country and confirmed via Eurydice.

**U.S. supplementary sources:** For grade 3 in the United States, where state-of-residence is not reported in TIMSS, the Early Childhood Longitudinal Study (ECLS) is used. The ECLS follows a kindergarten cohort from 1998 through spring 2002 (mostly grade 3). After sample restrictions (complete data on sex, birth date, cutoff date, test scores, and socioeconomic controls), 6,091 observations remain. For grade 8 in the United States, the National Education Longitudinal Study (NELS, 1988 cohort) is used; after restricting to states with a single unambiguous cutoff date and excluding states with mid-month cutoffs, 15,155 observations remain.

**British Columbia administrative data:** A comprehensive restricted-use panel compiled by the BC Ministry of Education via Edudata Canada, covering all public school students enrolled annually from 1995–2003. The analysis tracks students who entered grade 9 for the first time in 1996–1998 (followed for five years), yielding N = 106,917. ESL and disabled students are excluded before data delivery. No information is available for students who leave the BC school system.

**Key variables across all datasets:**
- `R` — assigned relative age (integer months, 0 = youngest eligible birth month, 11 = oldest eligible birth month)
- `A` — observed age in months (test month minus birth month)
- Math and science test scores (standardized to mean 50, SD 10)
- Socioeconomic controls: sex, grade, rural location, native-born mother and father, child lives with both parents, owns calculator/computer/more than 100 books, parental education (grade 8 only), household size
- University-bound indicator (BC): grade 12 completion within 5 years of entering grade 9 + ≥4 examinable subjects + ≥75% average in examinable courses
- SAT/ACT participation and accredited 4-year college enrollment (NELS waves 2 and 3, 1992 and 1994)

**Sample restrictions and data quality:** Countries with substantial rates of early or late entry (other than the four "clean" countries) have a minority of students ahead of or behind their assigned grade. This generates a confound for OLS but is addressed by the IV strategy. Missing socioeconomic controls are replaced with zeros, with accompanying missing-data dummies, to preserve sample size.

## Methodology

### Identification strategy

A single annual school cutoff date assigns children to cohorts based on birth month. Children born just after the cutoff are the oldest in their class (assigned relative age R = 11); children born just before the cutoff are the youngest (R = 0). The gap in age between the oldest and youngest is nearly twelve months, making the oldest approximately 20% older on the first day of school. Because birth timing is largely independent of parental strategic choices — verified empirically in Table II by showing births are roughly uniformly distributed across school quarters, with no systematic difference by maternal education — assigned relative age R is treated as exogenous.

Critically, this paper exploits the cross-national variation in cutoff dates (September in England, January in France, April in Greece, etc.) to separate relative age effects from season-of-birth effects. A child born in, say, October has a high relative age in England (cutoff September 1) but a low relative age in France (cutoff January 1). Including month-of-birth indicators in a pooled cross-country regression therefore controls for any direct seasonal effects on test scores while preserving the relative age variation.

### Estimator

The paper uses 2SLS (referred to as TSLS), estimated separately for each country c and grade g, with assigned relative age R as the instrument for observed age A.

**Equation (1) — OLS (biased baseline):**

$$S_{cgi} = \alpha_{cg} + \beta_{cg} A_{cgi} + X_{cgi}' \gamma_{cg} + \varepsilon_{cgi}$$

**Equation (2) — First stage:**

$$A_{cgi} = \delta_{1cg} + \delta_{2cg} R_{cgi} + X_{cgi}' \delta_{3cg} + \eta_{cgi}$$

**Equation (3) — Reduced form (key policy parameter):**

$$S_{cgi} = \theta_{1cg} + \theta_{2cg} R_{cgi} + X_{cgi}' \theta_{3cg} + u_{cgi}$$

The coefficient $\theta_{2cg}$ is the reduced-form impact: the effect on test scores of being assigned one month older relative age, net of grade retention and late entry. It is the policy-relevant parameter because it captures the total impact of the cutoff rule at a specific grade level. The IV estimate $\hat{\beta}_{cg} = \hat{\theta}_{2cg} / \hat{\delta}_{2cg}$ recovers the within-grade causal impact of one additional month of observed age, purging the confound of retention.

**Equation (4) — Cross-country pooled specification (season-of-birth control):**

$$S_{cgi} = \gamma_{1cg} + \gamma_{2g} R_{cgi} + \gamma_{3g} A_{cgi} + X_{cgi}' \gamma_{4g} + v_{cgi}$$

where $\gamma_{1cg}$ is a vector of country fixed effects and the model includes a full set of birth-month indicators (capturing season-of-birth effects common across countries). Row C further adds absolute school entry age controls.

**British Columbia long-run model (Equation 5 — reduced form):**

$$C_{syi} = \kappa_{1s} + \kappa_{2y} + \kappa_3 R_{syi} + \kappa_4 F_{syi} + v_{syi}$$

where $\kappa_{1s}$ are school fixed effects (school of record in grade 9), $\kappa_{2y}$ are grade-9 entry year indicators, and $F_{syi}$ is a female indicator. All long-run models are linear probability models with heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors.

### Target parameter and interpretation

The reduced-form coefficient $\theta_{2cg}$ is the average effect of being assigned one additional month of relative age on test scores at grade g in country c. Multiplied by 11 (the full range of R), it gives the total test score advantage of the oldest cohort member over the youngest — the quantity reported in percentile terms throughout. The IV coefficient gives the within-grade causal effect of one month of additional observed age, purging the retention/late-entry channel.

### Fixed effects and identifying variation

Country and grade fixed effects absorb cross-national differences in average test score levels. Month-of-birth indicators in the pooled model absorb any direct seasonal effects. Identification comes from within-country variation in R across birth months, and in the cross-country pooled model, from the fact that the same calendar birth month maps to different relative ages in different countries.

### Threats and robustness

The main threat to identification is strategic birth timing: if parents target favorable birth months (just after the cutoff), children with high relative age would be positively selected on unobservable ability. The paper finds no systematic evidence of this — birth distributions across school quarters are nearly uniform, and differences between more- and less-educated mothers are small and unsystematic (only 7 out of 60 education-group comparisons are significant, with no consistent pattern). A second concern is confounding with season-of-birth health effects; this is directly addressed by the cross-country pooled specification including month-of-birth dummies. The large downward OLS bias (or even negative estimates in high-retention countries) is itself evidence that simple age–outcome comparisons are severely confounded. Robustness checks include: alternative university-bound definitions for BC; specifications with and without socioeconomic controls; restricting the Italian sample to grade 7; excluding New Zealand from the pooled model (seasonality reversal in southern hemisphere).
