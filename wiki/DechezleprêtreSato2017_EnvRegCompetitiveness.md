# The Impacts of Environmental Regulations on Competitiveness

**Dechezleprêtre, Antoine & Misato Sato (2017)**
*Review of Environmental Economics and Policy*, 11(2):183–206
[Paper Link](https://doi.org/10.1093/reep/rex013)

---

## Abstract

This article reviews the recent empirical literature that attempts to quantify the effects of asymmetric environmental regulations on key aspects of firms' competitiveness, including trade, industry location, employment, productivity, and innovation. The first major review on this topic (Jaffe et al. 1995) concluded that there is relatively little evidence that environmental policies lead to large losses in competitiveness. Over the last two decades, both the growth in the number of environmental policies worldwide and the availability of high-quality data, especially at the firm and facility level, have enabled researchers to improve their empirical analyses of the economic effects of asymmetric environmental policies. The aim is to critically assess this evidence to ascertain whether the conclusions of Jaffe et al. (1995) still hold or should be updated.

---

## Research Question

What is the empirical evidence on whether asymmetric environmental policies lead to competitive disadvantages for regulated firms, through their effects on trade flows, industry location, employment, productivity, and innovation? This is a literature review — no single identification strategy is used; the paper evaluates a range of quasi-experimental and panel-data designs across an extensive body of empirical work.

---

## Results

The evidence on each competitiveness dimension can be summarised as follows:

**Trade:** Modest support for a pollution haven effect in trade. Levinson and Taylor (2008) find a 1% increase in US pollution abatement costs raises net imports from Mexico and Canada by 0.4–0.6%. Energy price differences account for only 0.01–0.2% of bilateral trade variation. Effects are small and concentrated in energy-intensive sectors.

**Industry location and FDI:** Evidence is mixed and highly sensitive to specification, data, and stringency measure. Significant effects found only for a few sectors (chemicals, primary metals) and specific country pairs. No consensus on pollution haven for FDI broadly.

**Employment:** Short-run negative effects documented in regulated (nonattainment) US counties — around 3.4% of manufacturing employment lost under 1970 CAAA (Greenstone 2002) — but general equilibrium effects may reduce net national impact. Revenue-neutral carbon taxes show distributional but not necessarily aggregate employment losses.

**Productivity:** Short-run negative TFP effects in some sectors (e.g., –4.8% in nonattainment counties, Greenstone et al. 2012). Positive medium-run effects documented in others. Evidence that highly productive firms gain more from regulations, while less productive firms suffer.

**Innovation:** Robust positive relationship: stricter regulation induces R&D and green patents. Consistent with induced innovation hypothesis. Firms subject to more stringent EU ETS rules innovate more in clean technologies.

**Overall:** Environmental regulations have small but real competitiveness effects. The pollution haven hypothesis finds some empirical support but the magnitude is modest. The Porter hypothesis (stricter regulation boosts competitiveness through innovation) finds partial empirical support. Effects remain overwhelmed by other determinants of trade and investment location.

## Data

This is a literature review — no primary dataset is constructed or analysed.

- **Type:** Systematic review of ex post evaluation studies
- **Scope:** Manufacturing sector studies in the US, Europe, and Asia–Pacific
- **Period covered:** Early 1970s (US Clean Air Act) through ~2016
- **Key studies reviewed:** Greenstone (2002), Greenstone, List & Syverson (2012), Levinson & Taylor (2008), Kahn & Mansur (2013), Deschenes (2011), Walker (2013), Albrizio et al. (2014), Rubashkina et al. (2015), Yamazaki (2017), Jaffe & Palmer (1997), and many others
- **Exclusions:** Ex ante modelling studies, agriculture/fisheries/forestry regulations, non-manufacturing contexts

---

## Methodology

The paper organises the empirical literature along two main theories (pollution haven hypothesis vs. Porter hypothesis) and five outcome dimensions (trade, location/FDI, employment, productivity, innovation). For each dimension it discusses:

- The theoretical channel (via first-, second-, and third-order effects of asymmetric regulation on relative production costs)
- The main identification challenges (endogeneity of stringency, measurement error in PACE-based proxies, omitted variable bias)
- The data advances enabling credible identification (firm/plant-level panel data, nonattainment county designs, EU ETS quasi-experiments, energy price variation)
- The magnitude and robustness of estimated effects across studies

The authors emphasise that the use of disaggregated (firm/facility-level) data and quasi-experimental designs has substantially improved credibility relative to earlier sector-level analyses, while many results remain sensitive to the choice of stringency proxy. Composite stringency indices and PACE are flagged as problematic (non-comparable across countries, endogenous to production levels, based on survey data).

## Relevance to Project

This review is directly relevant to the project's core research question on environmental provisions (EPs) in Chinese PTAs. It establishes the empirical background on the **pollution haven hypothesis**: whether differences in environmental stringency across countries shift pollution-intensive production and investment to lower-regulation jurisdictions. The project's central question is whether EPs in PTAs mitigate this dynamic. Dechezleprêtre & Sato (2017) confirm that pollution haven effects exist but are modest, providing the empirical motivation for studying whether EP provisions can meaningfully alter trade and investment flows.
